## Related Work

Research on LLM-based agents has grown along two largely independent tracks: multi-agent orchestration frameworks that propose new topologies for coordinating agents, and evaluation benchmarks that measure agent capabilities on standardized tasks. A third body of work explores inter-agent communication protocols, particularly debate. We organize prior work along these lines and show that no existing study bridges framework topology with systematic cross-benchmark evaluation --- the gap OrchestraBench addresses.

### 2.1 Multi-Agent Orchestration Topologies

The past two years produced at least eighteen distinct multi-agent frameworks, each committing to a particular orchestration topology. AutoGen \cite{wu_2023} pioneered the conversation-based paradigm, where customizable agents coordinate through multi-turn dialogue with humans and tools. Its flat topology treats every agent as an equal participant in an open-ended conversation loop, making it flexible but unstructured. MetaGPT \cite{hong_2023} took the opposite approach: it encodes Standardized Operating Procedures into an assembly-line pipeline, assigning specialist agents to sequential phases with intermediate verification gates. This hierarchical SOP topology achieves 85.9\% Pass@1 on code generation but demands domain expertise for SOP design. CAMEL \cite{li_2023} introduced inception prompting for role-playing agents, generating 25,000 cooperative dialogues between persona-assigned LLMs. ChatDev \cite{qian_2024} extended role-playing into a virtual software company simulation with chat-chain orchestration and communicative dehallucination, applying the same topology to end-to-end software development.

These four systems span three of the five canonical topologies we study --- flat conversation, hierarchical SOP, and role-playing --- yet none compares its topology against alternatives on the same tasks. Each framework paper evaluates only its own design on its own benchmarks, making cross-topology comparison impossible. The implicit assumption underlying each paper is that its chosen topology generalizes, but this assumption has never been tested.

More recent work has explored adaptive and learned orchestration. DyLAN \cite{liu_2024} automatically selects agent teams using an unsupervised importance score, achieving up to 25\% accuracy improvement on MMLU subsets. AFlow \cite{aflow_2025} automates workflow generation through Monte Carlo Tree Search over code-level modifications, outperforming hand-designed agent pipelines by 5.7\% and automated alternatives by 19.5\%. Evolving Orchestration \cite{evolving_2025} trains an RL-based puppeteer to dynamically sequence agent contributions based on evolving task states --- representing the RL-orchestrated topology in our taxonomy. MDAgents \cite{kim_2024} adapts collaboration structure based on medical task complexity, assigning solo or group configurations, but remains domain-specific and tests only two structural options rather than a full topology spectrum. Each of these systems introduces its own evaluation protocol, compounding the comparability problem.

Surveys of the landscape consistently identify the same blind spot. Wang et al.\ \cite{wang_2023} proposed a unified agent framework covering profile, memory, planning, and action modules without empirically comparing architectures. Subsequent surveys catalog coordination patterns, communication channels, and collaboration modalities across frameworks but provide no experimental cross-comparison. The collective limitation is consistent: each framework validates its own topology in isolation, and no paper controls for LLM backbone, tool access, and token budget while varying only the orchestration structure.

### 2.2 Task Characterization in Agent Benchmarks

A parallel research track has built increasingly sophisticated benchmarks for evaluating LLM agents, each implicitly targeting different task-structure characteristics. AgentBench \cite{liu_2023} established a multi-dimensional evaluation across 8 environments, testing 29 LLMs and revealing large performance gaps between commercial and open-source models on reasoning and decision-making tasks. SWE-bench \cite{jimenez_2024} created 2,294 real-world GitHub issue resolution tasks from 12 Python repositories with Docker-isolated execution and unit test verification --- tasks that demand high decomposability, as agents must localize bugs across files, write patches, and run tests in separable stages. WebArena \cite{zhou_2024} provides 812 long-horizon web navigation tasks across e-commerce, forums, and content management, requiring diverse tool interactions (clicking, form-filling, navigation, data extraction) within a self-contained environment where the best GPT-4 agent achieves 14.41\% versus 78.24\% human performance. GAIA \cite{mialon_2023} tests fundamental AI assistant abilities with 466 real-world questions spanning reasoning, web browsing, and multi-modal understanding; GPT-4 with plugins scored only 15\% versus 92\% for humans, indicating that these tasks demand extended iterative reasoning chains that current systems struggle to sustain.

These benchmarks occupy distinct regions of what we formalize as task-structure space. SWE-bench clusters at high decomposability with moderate tool diversity. WebArena clusters at high tool diversity with moderate decomposability. GAIA clusters at high iterativeness with low decomposability. This natural variation makes the three benchmarks complementary probes for topology-task alignment --- a property no prior work exploits. Mohammadi et al.\ \cite{mohammadi_2025} organize agent evaluation along two dimensions --- objectives (behavior, capabilities, reliability) and process (datasets, metrics, tooling) --- but explicitly flag orchestration evaluation as underexplored.

The critical observation: every benchmark evaluates single-agent configurations or one framework's multi-agent setup. None tests the same tasks under multiple orchestration topologies. The task-structure variation across benchmarks is an untapped resource for studying when and why particular topologies excel.

### 2.3 Topology-Agnostic Evaluation Efforts

The closest precedent for cross-topology comparison comes from multi-agent debate research. Du et al.\ \cite{du_2023} showed that multiple LLM instances debating and revising responses over rounds improves factual accuracy and mathematical reasoning relative to single-agent baselines. ReConcile \cite{chen_2024b} enhanced this paradigm with confidence-weighted voting across diverse LLMs, surpassing prior baselines by up to 11.4\% on reasoning benchmarks.

Smit et al.\ \cite{smit_2024} then challenged these claims directly. Their systematic benchmark of multi-agent debate (MAD) strategies found that most debate frameworks do not consistently outperform chain-of-thought \cite{wei_2022} or self-consistency baselines on the evaluated tasks. This negative result is significant for our work. We reinterpret it not as evidence against multi-agent systems generally, but as a predictable consequence of topology-task misalignment: debate imposes a high-iterativeness structure on tasks that may not benefit from adversarial refinement. Critically, the debate literature compares communication protocols within a single topology class (flat multi-agent conversation). It does not compare structurally different topologies --- hierarchical vs.\ flat vs.\ DAG-based --- on the same tasks, which is precisely what OrchestraBench does.

Single-agent reasoning methods provide the baselines against which any multi-agent topology must justify its coordination overhead. Chain-of-thought prompting \cite{wei_2022} established that intermediate reasoning steps improve performance at sufficient model scale. Tree of Thoughts \cite{yao_2023b} extended this into branching exploration with self-evaluation and backtracking, improving Game of 24 success from 4\% (CoT) to 74\%. ReAct \cite{yao_2023a} interleaved reasoning traces with tool actions for grounded decision-making across QA and fact verification tasks. Reflexion \cite{shinn_2023} added verbal self-correction across trials via episodic memory. Plan-and-Act \cite{erdogan_2025} separates a trained planner from an executor, achieving state-of-the-art 57.58\% success on WebArena-Lite. These methods demonstrate that a single well-prompted agent can be surprisingly competitive across task categories --- a finding our flat conversation baseline confirms empirically.

On the tool-use front, Toolformer \cite{schick_2023} showed LLMs can self-learn API invocation in a self-supervised manner, and ToolLLM \cite{qin_2024} scaled this to 16,464 real-world APIs with depth-first search-based decision trees. Both works address tool selection and grounding but exclusively for single agents. None examines how multiple agents should coordinate shared tool access under different orchestration structures --- precisely the dimension our tool diversity metric (T) captures.

### 2.4 Positioning: From Ad-Hoc Selection to Principled Alignment

The literature reveals a striking structural gap. Across our corpus of 54 papers, the multi-agent frameworks cluster (18 papers) and the evaluation benchmarks cluster (6 papers) share zero cross-cluster citation edges. No paper references works from both clusters simultaneously. Frameworks validate internally; benchmarks evaluate single agents or at most one framework's configuration. Practitioners selecting an orchestration topology for a new deployment have zero empirical guidance for the decision.

Two recent efforts approach --- but do not close --- this gap. MDAgents \cite{kim_2024} switches between solo and group collaboration for medical tasks, demonstrating that topology selection matters, but tests only two configurations in one domain. MAST \cite{cemri_2025} identifies 14 failure modes across 7 multi-agent frameworks, providing the first cross-framework failure analysis --- but diagnoses problems post-hoc without comparing topologies under controlled conditions or testing whether a different topology would have succeeded where the original failed.

OrchestraBench differs from all prior work in three respects. First, it implements five canonical topologies --- flat conversation, hierarchical SOP, role-playing, DAG-based, and RL-orchestrated --- as controlled wrappers sharing identical LLM backbone, tool access, and token budget, isolating the topology variable that every prior comparison confounds. Second, it evaluates across three established benchmarks (SWE-bench, WebArena, GAIA) spanning distinct regions of task-structure space, whereas every framework paper evaluates on at most one benchmark type. Third, it formalizes the relationship between task structure and topology effectiveness through three measurable dimensions --- decomposability (D), iterativeness (I), and tool diversity (T) --- enabling predictive topology selection rather than post-hoc performance reporting. Where Smit et al.\ \cite{smit_2024} found that debate "does not consistently help," OrchestraBench supplies the theoretical machinery to predict *when* each topology helps and *why*.
