The multi-agent orchestration field offers practitioners dozens of frameworks and no principled basis for choosing among them. We addressed that gap through three contributions.

First, we formalized the relationship between orchestration topologies and the tasks they serve. The D-I-T characterization (decomposability, iterativeness, tool diversity) provides a compact, measurable description of task structure that exposes the implicit assumptions every topology carries. This turns topology selection from guesswork into geometry.

Second, OrchestraBench delivered a controlled comparison isolating orchestration structure from model choice, tool access, and prompt design. Three topologies evaluated on 82 custom tasks across coding, reasoning, and research categories produced 794 total runs (738 three-seed primary runs on Claude Opus 4.6, 36 secondary backbone runs on Sonnet 4.6, and 20 agent-count control runs) under identical conditions. No single topology dominates; each topology's advantage concentrates where its structural prior matches the task. Hierarchical reaches 75\% on high-decomposability tasks while debate reaches 77\% on high-iterativeness tasks (3-seed means), and flat conversation collapses on hard tasks regardless of profile.

Third, a DIT routing classifier selects the best topology with 90\% accuracy (bootstrap 95\% CI: [84\%, 95\%]), nearly tripling the 33\% random baseline. Topology effectiveness tracks a measurable geometric property, not merely model capability or engineering effort. The failure modes are structural mirrors: hierarchical fails on high-I tasks by severing cross-cutting concerns, debate fails on high-D tasks by producing divergent designs that resist merging. Each topology's weakness is the other's strength, and DIT coordinates predict which regime applies.

Orchestration topology is not a detail to be decided by convention. It is a first-class design variable with measurable impact, and the tools to choose it well now exist.
